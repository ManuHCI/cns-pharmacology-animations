Why Diffusion Hypoxia Happens
① Massive reverse flux of N₂O N₂O has been given at 70% for the duration of the case → blood is saturated with N₂O (its low solubility means it stores in plasma not tissues, but volume is huge). When inspired N₂O = 0, the gradient reverses → N₂O floods OUT of blood INTO alveolus in seconds.
② Alveolar O₂ is diluted The bolus of N₂O coming up displaces alveolar gas. Room air supplies only 21% O₂ — not enough to replace what's being diluted. Alveolar PO₂ falls → arterial PO₂ falls → SpO₂ drops (often to 85–90%, sometimes lower).
③ Alveolar CO₂ is also diluted Same N₂O bolus dilutes CO₂ → ↓ PaCO₂ → respiratory drive blunted → patient hypoventilates → hypoxia worsens.
④ Prevention Administer 100% O₂ for 5–10 minutes after stopping N₂O. The high inspired O₂ replaces what's diluted; the patient washes out N₂O safely.
Coined by Bernard Raymond Fink (1955) Hence "Fink effect." Most clinically relevant in the immediate post-anaesthesia minutes — especially in elderly, obese, or pulmonary-disease patients with low O₂ reserves.
High-Yield Recap
  • Same physics as 2nd-gas effect — just in reverse direction (blood→alveolus instead of alveolus→blood)
  • Largest risk window: first 5–10 min after N₂O off
  • Other low-solubility agents (Xenon, Desflurane) — similar but less, since not given at 70%
  • Not seen with halothane/iso/sevo alone — they're given at <5% so reverse flux is small
  • Closely related: avoid N₂O in any closed gas space (bowel obstruction, pneumothorax, eye Sx, ear Sx)
