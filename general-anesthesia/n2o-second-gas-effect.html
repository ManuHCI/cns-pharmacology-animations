Two Mechanisms — Second Gas Effect
① Concentrating Effect N₂O is given in high concentration (70%). Because of its low blood:gas solubility (λ = 0.47), enormous volumes are rapidly absorbed into pulmonary blood. Alveolar volume contracts → remaining gases (including second gas) become more concentrated per unit volume.
② Augmented Inflow Effect Volume contraction creates negative alveolar pressure → extra fresh gas is drawn in from the airway. This inflow contains BOTH N₂O and the second gas → more second gas molecules arrive per breath.
Net Result Alveolar FA/FI of the second gas rises faster than if given alone → faster induction of anaesthesia.
Sister effect: Concentration Effect The SAME rapid-uptake principle, applied to N₂O itself. The higher the inspired N₂O concentration, the faster its own alveolar concentration rises. (Robert Eger, 1963.)
Clinical Pearls
  • N₂O + Sevo / Iso / Halo — classical 2nd-gas pair for faster induction
  • Blood:gas λ — N₂O 0.47 · Sevo 0.65 · Iso 1.4 · Halo 2.3
  • Diffusion hypoxia — opposite at end: N₂O exits fast → dilutes alveolar O₂ → give 100% O₂ for 5–10 min
  • Closed gas spaces — N₂O enters faster than N₂ exits → expansion (bowel, pneumothorax, middle ear). Avoid.
  • MAC of N₂O = 104% — cannot be used alone at 1 atm; always with adjuncts
